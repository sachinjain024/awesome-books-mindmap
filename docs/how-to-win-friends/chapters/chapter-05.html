Smile

Part 2: Six Ways to Make People Like You — Principle 5

“A smile says: I like you. You make me happy. I am glad to see you.” — Dale Carnegie

The Power of a Simple Smile

Carnegie devotes an entire chapter to the smile—and on first reading, this seems almost trivially obvious. Of course people should smile. But Carnegie is making a more subtle and important point than “be cheerful.” He is exploring the relationship between outward expression and inner state, and arguing that a smile is not just a symptom of happiness but a cause of it.

The ancient Chinese understood this. An old Chinese proverb says: “A man without a smiling face must not open a shop.” The value of a smile in any transaction—commercial, social, or personal—is almost impossible to overstate. But the smile Carnegie is talking about is not a salesman’s manufactured grin. It is the expression of genuine warmth and welcome.

The Difference Between Real and Fake Smiles

In the 1860s, French neurologist Guillaume Duchenne identified what scientists now call the “Duchenne smile”—a genuine smile that engages not just the mouth muscles but also the muscles around the eyes. When you genuinely smile, your eyes crinkle and light up. When you fake a smile, only your mouth moves.

People detect this difference unconsciously and immediately. A forced smile often creates more discomfort than no smile at all—it signals performance rather than feeling. Carnegie’s point is that the only smile worth having is a real one.

What Produces a Real Smile?

Carnegie’s answer: genuine thoughts and genuine feelings. If you think about someone you love—really picture them, really feel warmth toward them—your face will naturally express that warmth. If you think about all the things you dislike about a meeting before you walk in, your face will express that, too.

The practical implication is that changing how you feel begins with changing what you think about. Before entering a room, consciously call to mind something you genuinely like about the people you’re about to meet, or something you’re genuinely grateful for. Your face will follow.

Act As If: The William James Principle

William James’s insight—that action and feeling, while we cannot directly change the feeling, we can indirectly change it by changing the action—is central to Carnegie’s approach to smiling.

James observed that we don’t smile because we’re happy; we’re happy because we smile. The relationship between expression and emotion runs both ways. Studies in modern psychology have confirmed this: forcing your face into a smile, even artificially, elevates mood in measurable ways. Holding a pencil between your teeth (forcing a smile-like expression) makes cartoons seem funnier.

This is not Carnegie saying “fake it until you make it” in the cynical sense. It is a genuine insight into how human psychology works: we can cultivate positive emotional states by deliberately practicing the expressions and behaviors associated with them.

The Act-As-If Exercise

Carnegie recounts the story of a New York stockbroker who used this principle to transform his professional and personal life. The man was naturally taciturn and serious. He decided to practice acting as if he were happy—smiling at people, greeting them warmly, behaving as a happy person would behave. Within a few weeks, he discovered that the act had become natural. The performance had become reality.

“Act as if you were already happy,” Carnegie writes, “and that will tend to make you happy.” This is a profound observation about the nature of character: we do not wait for the feeling before we act; we act our way into a new feeling.

The Telephone Smile

Carnegie tells the story of a large telephone company in New York that studied why some operators consistently received better ratings from customers than others. They discovered that the operators people liked most were the ones who smiled—even though, of course, the customer on the other end of the line couldn’t see them.

The smile was audible. It shaped the tone of voice, the pace of speech, the quality of attention. People can hear whether you’re smiling, even through a telephone. This extends to email, text messages, and every other medium of communication: the emotional state you are in when you compose a message shapes how it reads.

Smiling in Every Medium

The principle of the smile extends beyond face-to-face interaction:

  • Phone calls: A genuine smile changes your vocal tone and makes you sound warmer and more engaged
  • Emails: Taking a moment to think warmly about the recipient before writing changes what you write and how
  • Meetings: Walking in already mentally engaged and positive changes the entire dynamic
  • Video calls: Your face takes up most of the screen; your expression shapes the entire conversation

The Value You Create

Carnegie closes this chapter with a beautiful observation about the economics of a smile. “A smile costs nothing but creates much. It enriches those who receive it without impoverishing those who give it. It happens in a flash and the memory of it sometimes lasts forever.”

This framing captures something important: most of what we give to others costs us something—time, energy, money, attention. A smile costs nothing and yet it can make someone’s day, deepen a relationship, or transform a difficult interaction. It is perhaps the highest return-on-investment action available to any human being.

Daily Practice: The Smile Intention

For one week:

  1. Before any significant interaction (a meeting, a phone call, walking into a room), pause for five seconds and deliberately call to mind something genuine you feel warmly about
  2. Let your face naturally express that warmth
  3. Notice the effect on how the interaction goes
  4. At the end of the day, reflect on which smiles felt real and which felt forced—and what was different about those moments

Reflection

Is there someone in your life with whom you have fallen into a pattern of greeting coldly or neutrally? What would change if, the next time you saw them, you smiled genuinely—really thought about something you appreciate about them, and let that show on your face?

Key Takeaways

  • A genuine smile is one of the most powerful tools in human relations—simple, free, and universally understood
  • The Duchenne smile (engaging the eyes as well as the mouth) is instantly distinguished from a fake smile; authenticity matters
  • The relationship between expression and emotion runs both ways: acting happy can make you happy
  • Even over the phone, a smile is audible—it shapes your voice and your entire manner
  • Carnegie’s smile principle is not about performance but about cultivating genuine warmth and then expressing it freely
  • “It enriches those who receive it without impoverishing those who give it”—a smile costs nothing and creates enormous value
